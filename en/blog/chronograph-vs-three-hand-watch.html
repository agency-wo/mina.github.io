Chronograph or Three Hands? What Those Subdials Actually Do

Not every watch on our counter with subdials is a chronograph. Some of ours time something. Others only look like they do, and show the day, the date and 24-hour time instead. Here is how to tell in five seconds, and which one is worth your money.

11 min read Iglisi Watch - Durrës August 2026

Walk along our counter and many watches have small dials set inside the main one. They look like the same idea. They are not. On some of them those small dials are a stopwatch. On the others they show the day, the date and the time on a 24-hour scale, and nothing on the watch times anything at all. Both are honest watches, and both are worth owning. Only one of them does what most people assume it does, and it takes five seconds to check.

How to Tell in Five Seconds

Three checks, fastest first. You can do all of them across a shop counter without asking anyone.

  • Read the dial. The Navimarine multifunction models print MULTIFUNCTION on the dial. If the word is there, the small dials are a calendar and that is the end of it.
  • Read the small scales. A chronograph subdial counts to 30 or to 60, because it is measuring minutes and seconds. A multifunction subdial is marked with the days of the week, or runs to 24. Days of the week are unmistakable.
  • Press the top pusher. On a chronograph a hand starts to sweep, and pressing again stops it. On a multifunction either there is nothing to press or the button only steps the calendar forward.

The one that catches people is the Bigotti BG1.10154.1 at 84 euro (8,100 L). Its case is a three-pusher chronograph case, so from the side it looks like a chronograph. The dial is multifunction, with day and date. That is not a fault and it is not a fake, it is a case design a lot of brands use, and the watch is good value at the price. It is simply worth knowing before you press anything. The other Bigotti models are three-hand dress watches.

One more marking worth recognising. If the outer ring carries a descending scale from around 400 down to 60, that is a tachymeter, and it only means anything with a working chronograph behind it. Our guide to bezel types explains what it is for.

Hislon Classic or Hislon Masterwork: Same Money, Opposite Dials

The clearest way to see what a chronograph costs you is to put two watches from the same brand side by side.

Hislon Classic at 199 euro (19,300 L) has a sapphire crystal, a fluted bezel, a silver dial with fine horizontal striping, a date at three, and nothing else. It slips under a shirt cuff without catching.

Hislon Masterwork at 184 euro (17,800 L) has the same sapphire crystal, a jet-black dial, three working subdials with red accents, and a date. It is the loudest watch in the cabinet, in the good sense.

Fifteen euro apart. Same brand, same crystal, same 1-year guarantee, both new. The extra money is not buying more watch. It is buying a different dial. That is the whole decision, which is why we tell people to stop reading specifications at that point and wear both for a minute.

More Dial Is Not More Information

Three subdials sit exactly where the hour markers would otherwise be. Something has to give, and it is usually the markers at 6, 9 and 3. On most chronographs the running seconds hand also moves off the main dial and into one of the small circles, so the question of whether the watch is still going is answered in a space about eight millimetres across.

What rescues a busy dial is contrast. Hislon Masterwork uses red on the registers so your eye separates them from the hands instantly. Philippe Lauren Steel Chronograph at 72 euro (7,000 L) uses luminous applied indices, which do the same job in the dark. A poorly balanced chronograph dial is one where three grey circles sit on a grey dial, and you will know within a day.

The opposite argument is on the same counter. Navimarine Steel 5ATM at 75 euro (7,300 L) and Daniel Klein Premium Roman at 69 euro (6,700 L) give you Roman numerals, two hands, a date and nothing competing. The test is one second at arm's length. If you cannot read the time in that second, no feature on the dial makes up for it.

Battery: the Cost of a Stopwatch You Never Use

A plain quartz watch here goes 2 to 3 years on a battery. A quartz chronograph with a date runs 1 to 2. Over ten years that is two or three extra trips to the counter, which is worth knowing but is not a large number.

The thing that genuinely drains a chronograph is leaving the stopwatch running. People start it, get distracted, and the watch keeps a second hand sweeping for a fortnight. Press the lower pusher and send it back to zero when you are done. That one habit is worth more than any battery brand.

A battery here is 200 to 600 Lek depending on which cell the watch takes, done while you wait, and we check the gasket while the back is off. There is more on how long a cell should last in our battery guide. None of this is a reason to choose one watch over the other. It is a reason to know what you are signing up for.

What Each One Costs to Look After

Every chronograph we stock is quartz, and that matters more than anything else in this section. A mechanical chronograph is a different animal, with several times the parts of a simple movement and a service interval to match. Our complications guide has that comparison. None of it applies to what is in our cabinet.

A quartz chronograph needs a battery and a gasket check, exactly like a three-hander. The one real difference is the pushers: each one is a hole through the side of the case with its own seal, so there are two more places water can get in than on a plain watch. We check them whenever the back is off. That is part of the job, not an extra.

Beyond the battery we do not publish prices, and that is deliberate. A pusher that has stiffened is not the same job as one that has not, and quoting a number before we have opened the watch would either overcharge you or trap us. We look first, we tell you what it costs, and then you decide.

So Which One Should You Buy?

  • You will genuinely time something. Buy a real chronograph. The cheapest is Philippe Lauren Black Square Chronograph at 69 euro (6,700 L), the best built is Hislon Masterwork at 184 euro (17,800 L).
  • You want the busy dial and nothing else. The multifunction models cost less than the chronographs at the same size and they tell you the day, which you will read far more often than you will time an egg. Navimarine NT0029-2 at 85 euro (8,200 L) is the pick of them.
  • You want to read the time and stop thinking about it. Navimarine NMM1011 at 60 euro (5,800 L) or Navimarine Steel 5ATM at 75 euro (7,300 L).
  • You cannot decide. Come in and wear one of each for two minutes. Nobody has ever left that undecided.

There is no wrong answer here, only a wrong reason. Buying a chronograph because you thought all subdials were chronograph subdials is a wrong reason, and it is the one this article exists to prevent.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do the three small dials on my watch actually do anything?

It depends on the watch. On a chronograph they are a stopwatch: elapsed seconds, elapsed minutes, and the running seconds of the watch itself. On a multifunction watch they show the day, the date and the time on a 24-hour scale, and nothing on the watch times anything. Some of the watches we stock are real chronographs and others are multifunction. The quickest check is the dial itself: the multifunction models have MULTIFUNCTION printed on them.

How do I tell a real chronograph from a lookalike in the shop?

Three checks. Press the top pusher and watch for a hand starting to sweep. Read the small scales: a chronograph subdial counts to 30 or 60, a multifunction one is marked with the days of the week or runs to 24. And look for MULTIFUNCTION printed on the dial. The one that catches people is the Bigotti multifunction at 84 euro (8,100 L), because the case has three pushers like a chronograph while the dial is multifunction. That is a common case design, not a fault.

Does a chronograph go through batteries faster?

Yes, by a predictable amount. A plain quartz watch runs 2 to 3 years on a battery and a quartz chronograph with a date runs 1 to 2, so over ten years you are looking at two or three extra battery changes. What actually costs you is leaving the stopwatch running for days, so reset it when you are done. A battery here is 200 to 600 Lek depending on the cell, done while you wait, with a gasket check included. It is not a reason to choose one watch over the other.

Is a chronograph more expensive to service than a three-hand watch?

Not in the way people expect, because every chronograph we stock is quartz. That means a battery and a gasket check, not the full overhaul a mechanical chronograph would need. The one real difference is the pushers: each is a hole through the case with its own seal, so there are two more places water can get in, and we check them whenever the back is off. Beyond the battery we quote at the bench, after we have opened the watch and seen what it needs.

Are chronograph cases bigger than three-hand cases?

Yes, and by a noticeable margin. Chronograph cases run larger and thicker and the pushers add width on the right-hand side. Our Navimarine chronograph and multifunction models are 44 to 46mm while the classic range is 38 to 42mm, and the Hislon Classic is 38 to 40mm and under 9mm thick. Those are the case figures. We will measure any watch in the cabinet and send you the numbers before you order.

So which one should I buy?

If you will genuinely time something, buy a real chronograph: the cheapest is the Philippe Lauren Black Square at 69 euro (6,700 L) and the best built is the Hislon Masterwork at 184 euro (17,800 L). If you want the busy dial and nothing else, the multifunction models cost less and tell you the day. If you want to read the time and forget about it, the Navimarine NMM1011 at 60 euro (5,800 L).
