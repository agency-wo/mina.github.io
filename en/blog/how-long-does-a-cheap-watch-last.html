How Long Does a 70 Euro Watch Last? What We See on the Bench

Five to ten years, and the thing that decides where you land in that range is almost never the price. We repair watches ten minutes from the beach in Durrës, so here is what actually fails, in what order, and what a decade of ownership really costs.

8 min read Iglisi Watch - Durrës August 2026

Nobody standing at the counter with a 6,500 Lek watch is asking whether it looks good. They can see that. What they actually want to know, and usually ask sideways, is whether it will still be running in three years or whether they are buying something disposable. It is a fair question and it deserves a straight number rather than a sales answer, so here is ours: five to ten years. What follows is why that range is so wide, and what puts a watch at one end of it rather than the other.

The Short Answer: Five to Ten Years

A quartz movement in this price range is a genuinely reliable thing. There is no mainspring winding down, no oil film breaking under load, no escapement wearing itself out. It is a battery, a crystal oscillator and a tiny stepper motor, and none of those parts is under mechanical stress in the way a mechanical watch's are. That is the whole reason a cheap quartz watch can outlive a far more expensive mechanical one that has been neglected.

So five years is close to a floor for a watch that is worn normally and not abused, and ten is entirely achievable. We have a Casio A159WA at 5,600 Lek on the counter that has been in production essentially unchanged since the early 1980s, and we see them come in twenty years old, bracelet worn to a matte grey, still keeping better time than watches that cost fifty times more.

Where the range comes from. The difference between five years and ten is almost entirely about water, impact and whether anyone ever changes the battery before it leaks. It is not about the brand and it is not about the price. A carefully worn 6,500 Lek watch will outlast a carelessly worn 20,000 Lek one, every time.

What Actually Fails, In Order

This is the part that surprises people. Across the repairs that come through our bench, the order is consistent: the battery first, then the strap or bracelet, then the crown. The movement is not in the top three.

A battery is not a failure at all, it is scheduled maintenance, roughly every couple of years. Straps and bracelets are next because they are the only part of the watch in permanent contact with skin, sweat and everything you brush past in a day, and a bracelet pin is a wear part in exactly the way a shoelace is. Crowns come third, usually after being pulled out at an angle for years rather than from any defect.

On that twenty-year-old Casio, the commonest repair we see is not the movement at all. It is a worn bracelet pin, a part that costs less than a coffee. That is the honest shape of watch ownership at this price: the expensive-sounding component is the one that keeps working, and the cheap-sounding ones are what you occasionally replace.

What Kills Watches in Durrës

Our bench is ten minutes from the beach, which means we see the same damage every year on a schedule. Fogged crystals in July. Green corrosion around the crown in August. Dead movements in September. Almost none of it comes from bad watches. It comes from good watches taken into water they were never rated for.

This is the single most useful thing in this article, so it is worth stating flatly: when a movement at this price does die, it is nearly always water that killed it. Not age, not wear, not the fact that the watch was cheap. Salt water gets past a gasket that was fine for rain, sits inside the case, and three weeks later the movement is gone. The watch did not fail. It was taken somewhere it was not built for.

Two things follow. Check the rating printed on the dial before the watch goes near the sea, and if you want one that genuinely handles a swim, buy for that on purpose. The Navimarine Steel 5ATM Blue at 6,900 Lek has 5 ATM printed on it and is the one we point people at for the beach. Our summer and sea guide goes through the ratings properly.

What Ten Years Actually Costs

Here is the arithmetic, using the only service price we quote from a list rather than at the bench. A battery change is 200 to 600 Lek depending on the battery, and a quartz watch needs one roughly every couple of years.

So a Navimarine NM182-01 at 6,500 Lek, worn for ten years with five battery changes, costs somewhere between 7,800 and 9,800 Lek in total. That is under 1,000 Lek a year to own a steel watch with a fluted bezel and a jubilee bracelet. Even on the pessimistic five-year view it lands around 1,500 Lek a year.

The one thing worth doing. Change the battery before it dies, not after. A flat battery left in a case for months can leak, and cleaning that up is the one avoidable repair that turns a cheap watch into a write-off. If the second hand starts jumping in four-second steps, that is the movement telling you the battery is nearly out. Bring it in that week.

Where a 70 Euro Watch Genuinely Stops

Honesty is the point of this article, so here are the limits. The crystal is mineral glass, which resists scratches well but not as well as sapphire, and after some years of daily wear it will show fine marks under a light. Gold-tone is plating over steel, and plating wears at the clasp and the bracelet edges where it rubs. The water rating is whatever is printed on the dial and no more, however solid the watch feels.

None of that stops the watch working. It changes how it looks after year five. If you want a piece that still looks new at that point rather than merely running, that is a sapphire crystal, and on our counter that starts with the Hislon Women Classic Queen at 13,700 Lek. We wrote separately about what more money actually buys as you climb, and the crystal is the honest headline of it.

What Does Die Young

Everything above describes a real watch from a real brand. There is a separate category sold in tourist areas and on social media, and it behaves completely differently: no-name and counterfeit movements have a lifespan measured in months rather than years, in a case that cannot be opened cleanly and for which no part will ever be available.

That is the actual difference between cheap and bad. A 6,500 Lek Navimarine and a 6,500 Lek fake are not the same purchase at all, and the gap shows up in year one. If you are unsure what you are being offered, our guide on how to spot a fake watch covers what to check.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a 70 euro watch actually last?

Five to ten years in normal use, and that range is honest rather than cautious. The quartz movement itself is not the limiting factor: there is no mainspring under tension and no oil film to break down, so it simply keeps running as long as it gets a battery every couple of years. Where you land in that range depends on water, knocks and whether the battery is changed before it dies, not on what you paid.

What fails first on a cheap watch?

The battery, then the strap or bracelet, then the crown. That is the order we see at the bench and the movement is not in the top three. A battery is scheduled maintenance rather than a failure. Bracelets wear because they are the only part in constant contact with skin and sweat, and a bracelet pin is a wear part. Crowns usually go after years of being pulled out at an angle.

Does the movement itself ever die?

Almost never, and when it does it is nearly always water that killed it. Our bench is ten minutes from the beach in Durres and we see the same pattern every year: fogged crystals in July, corrosion around the crown in August, dead movements in September. That is not old watches wearing out, it is good watches taken into water they were never rated for. Check the rating on the dial before the sea, not after.

What does ten years of ownership cost?

A 6,500 Lek watch plus a battery every couple of years at 200 to 600 Lek comes to roughly 7,800 to 9,800 Lek over ten years, which is under 1,000 Lek a year. On a more cautious five-year view it works out around 1,500 Lek a year. The battery is the only routine cost a quartz watch has; everything else is repair rather than maintenance and we quote it before starting.

Is a 149 euro watch twice as durable?

Not in the sense of running twice as long, no. The Hislon Classic has a Swiss movement and a sapphire crystal, and the site says it runs well over ten years, but the real difference you notice is how it looks at year five rather than whether it still works. Sapphire resists the fine scratches that mineral glass picks up. If you want a watch that still looks new rather than merely runs, that is what the extra buys.

My watch has stopped. Is it worth repairing?

Usually it is a battery, which we change while you wait, and a stopped watch is rarely the disaster it feels like. If it turns out to be more than that we tell you what it is and what it will cost before we touch it, and occasionally the honest answer is that it is not worth repairing.
