What Sapphire Actually Buys You in Daily Wear

Hardness, and only hardness. Synthetic sapphire sits at about 9 on the Mohs scale, second to diamond, and in normal wear it does not scratch. Mineral glass sits around 5 to 6, which sounds close and is not: a door frame, keys in the same pocket, the edge of a desk you slide your wrist across every working day. Neither material can be polished once it is marked, so a hairline in mineral glass is permanent.

That is the whole practical difference and it is larger than it sounds, because the crystal is the part of a watch a stranger looks straight through. A scratched crystal is the single detail that makes a watch read as old across a table, more than a scuffed case and more than a tired strap. How to Tell Sapphire From Mineral Glass

Read the description first. We name a crystal only where we have verified it, so sapphire on our counter means the Romanson Parie or a Hislon. The only other crystal we name anywhere is on the Citizen Quartz GN at 60 euro (5,500 L), which says mineral glass. Everything else we sell carries no crystal claim from us at all, and a shop that names the crystal on every watch it lists is guessing on most of them.

Look for the sheen. Anti-reflective coating is applied almost exclusively to sapphire, and it shows as a faint blue or purple cast when you tilt the watch under a light.

Tap it with a fingernail. Acrylic sounds dull and plastic, mineral and sapphire both sound crisper. That separates plastic from glass, not mineral from sapphire, so it only takes you halfway.

Distrust the price rule. Sapphire does get more common as prices climb, but the rule is weaker than people think, and the Romanson is the reason we stopped repeating it.

Why 74 Euro Can Carry Both Sapphire and Swiss

A Swiss quartz movement is a component, not a luxury. Switzerland has built quartz movements in industrial volume since the 1970s, and what makes a Swiss watch expensive is rarely the movement in a quartz dress watch. It is the case work, the hand finishing, the marketing and the name. A watch can carry a Swiss quartz movement and a modest price without either fact being untrue. Our guide to Swiss and Japanese movements is the longer version of that argument.

Sapphire is cheap in small flat sizes and expensive in large shaped ones. Synthetic sapphire is grown in quantity and then sliced; the cost sits in the diameter, the doming and any shaping. A small flat crystal over a rectangular dress case is close to the cheapest sapphire a factory can fit, which is exactly why this watch has one and a large domed sport case at the same price would not.

Gold-plated is a finish over base metal. The description says gold-plated and not gold, which is the third saving and the one nobody should misunderstand.

What Gold-Plated Means, and What It Does Not

Gold plating is a thin layer of real gold deposited over a base metal. It is gold you can see and it is not gold you could weigh: the value is in the finish rather than the metal. It wears at the high points first, which on a bracelet watch means the clasp edges and the outer links, over years of daily wear rather than months.

The alternative wording is worth knowing too. The Hislon Classic in full gold-tone stainless steel at 189 euro (17,400 L) is described as gold-tone, which is a colour and not a plating claim. Neither sentence is dishonest and they are not the same sentence, so we publish whichever one is true for the watch in front of us. Our gold-tone watches guide walks the counter that way.

Sapphire Is Scratch-Resistant, Not Unbreakable

The hardness that shrugs off scratches is the same property that makes sapphire brittle. A hard concentrated impact, a watch dropped face-down onto tile or concrete, can chip or crack a sapphire crystal where mineral glass might have survived. Replacing one costs more as well: sapphire is sourced to order and priced by diameter and shape, and we quote before we order anything.
