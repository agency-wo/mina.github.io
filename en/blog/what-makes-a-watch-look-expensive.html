What Makes a Watch Look Expensive: Six Details That Do the Work

The expensive look is not vague. It is made of about six specific details, each of which started as a solution to a real problem before it became a signature. Here is where each one came from, and what it costs on our counter in Durrës.

9 min read Iglisi Watch - Durrës August 2026

People rarely walk in asking for a specific watch. They walk in with a picture in their head and say something like "I want one that looks serious" or "something that does not look cheap". After enough of those conversations you learn that the expensive look is not vague at all. It comes down to roughly six details, and every one of them started as an engineering answer to a real problem before it turned into a style. Knowing what they are is useful in both directions: it tells you what to look for, and it stops you paying for a name when what you actually wanted was a shape.

1. The Fluted Bezel

The ring of fine vertical grooves around the glass is the single most recognisable "expensive watch" cue, and it began as a tool grip. In the 1920s Rolex needed the bezel screwed hard onto the case to keep water out, so it was machined with teeth and tightened with a special tool, exactly like a bottle cap. Once cases were sealed by other means the teeth had no job left, and the brand kept them anyway because by then people recognised them. That is the whole history: a manufacturing necessity that outlived its own purpose and became a signature.

Because it is a machining pattern rather than a material, it is one of the few luxury cues that transfers honestly to an affordable watch. The Navimarine NM182-01 at 6,800 Lek carries a proper fluted bezel with a five-link bracelet. The Daniel Klein Premium at 6,200 Lek does it in gold-tone with a Roman numeral chapter ring, and the Belonni Collection at 4,900 Lek, the cheapest watch we stock, has one too. At the top of our range the Hislon Classic at 18,300 Lek has the same detail in full gold-tone steel with a sapphire crystal.

2. The Five-Link Jubilee Bracelet

The bracelet does more work than most buyers expect. A three-link bracelet reads as ordinary; five narrower links across the same width read as intricate, because there is simply more going on per centimetre. It was designed in 1945 for the launch of a new dress watch and named for the manufacturer's fortieth anniversary, which is where the word Jubilee comes from.

It is also genuinely more comfortable, since more links means smaller increments and a closer fit to the wrist. We have nine watches on this bracelet style, from the Belonni Collection at 4,900 Lek at the bottom to the Navimarine 001808 at 8,200 Lek with its 24-hour bezel. The Daniel Klein Premium Roman at 6,700 Lek pairs it with a cream Roman dial, which is about as dressy as this price gets.

3. The Sunburst Dial

Hold a plain painted dial under a light and it stays the same colour. Hold a sunburst dial and it changes as your wrist moves, going bright on one side and nearly black on the other. The effect comes from brushing the dial in fine lines radiating from the centre before it is coloured, so each line catches light at a different angle.

This is the cheapest expensive-looking detail there is, because it costs almost nothing to do and changes the watch completely. Sixteen of our watches have it. The Daniel Klein Blue at 6,200 Lek shows it most obviously in a deep blue gradient, and the Navimarine Steel 5ATM at 7,300 Lek pairs it with Roman numerals for something more formal.

A quick way to test any watch before you buy it. Tilt it slowly under the shop lights. A sunburst dial will shift through several shades. A flat painted dial will not move at all. It takes two seconds and it tells you how much care went into the piece.

4. The Blue Dial

Blue is the colour that has done the most for affordable watches in the last decade. For most of watchmaking history dials were black, white or silver, because those were the reliable finishes; a good blue is harder to get right and was for a long time a sign that someone had spent money on the dial. That is no longer true technically, but the association stuck, and a blue dial still reads as more considered than a black one.

We have seven. The range is wide: the Navimarine NMM1011 at 5,800 Lek is a pale ice-blue on a slim minimalist case with no date window at all, the Navimarine NM001404 in blue at 7,300 Lek is a deep blue sunburst behind an integrated bracelet, the Navimarine NM181-03 at 6,800 Lek is a proper sport watch with a rotating blue bezel and a yellow seconds hand, and the Daniel Klein Exclusive Chronograph at 8,600 Lek is a blue chronograph with three subdials. Four quite different watches, one colour doing a lot of work in each.

5. The Integrated Bracelet

This is the most modern cue on the list and the hardest to fake badly. On an ordinary watch the bracelet is a separate part attached between two lugs, and you can see the join. On an integrated design the bracelet flows out of the case as if the two were made as one object, with no visible break. Gerald Genta drew the first of them for Audemars Piguet in 1972 and again for Patek Philippe in 1976, and the shape has been the defining luxury sport-watch look ever since.

It is difficult to do cheaply because it demands the case and bracelet be designed and tolerated together, so when you see it done properly at this price it is worth noticing. The Navimarine NM001404 in blue at 7,300 Lek and its white-dialled sibling the Navimarine NM001404 in white at 8,700 Lek both carry it, and the Philippe Lauren Steel Sport at 6,200 Lek takes a different route with a faceted bezel and four visible screws.

6. Two-Tone Steel and Gold

Mixing gold with steel was an eighties idea that has come back properly in the last few years. It works because the contrast draws the eye along the bracelet and around the bezel, so the watch reads as more decorated than a single-colour piece even when the shapes are identical.

On our counter it is mostly the women's dress watches. The Navimarine NM282-04 at 6,300 Lek has a cushion-square case with a crystal-set bezel and a deep green dial, and the Navimarine NM268-06 at 8,200 Lek is the most jewellery-like watch we stock, with a blue sunburst dial and gold-tone Roman numerals. Further up, the Hislon Women Classic Queen at 14,500 Lek takes a quieter route: mother-of-pearl instead of gold, which is a natural material, so no two dials are ever quite the same.

What These Details Do Not Buy

Here is the part most articles like this leave out. Every cue above is cosmetic. None of them changes the crystal, the movement or the water resistance, which are the three things that actually decide how a watch survives ten years of daily wear. A fluted bezel on a mineral crystal is still a mineral crystal. A sunburst dial does not make a quartz movement more accurate.

That matters when you are deciding where your money goes. If you want the look, these details deliver it honestly and cheaply. If you want the watch to still look new in five years, that is a different purchase and it is mostly about the crystal. We wrote a separate piece on exactly that, on what more money actually buys as you climb the price ladder, and it is the honest companion to this one.

The Line Between a Design Cue and a Fake

This needs saying plainly, because it is the difference between a good purchase and a bad one. A fluted bezel is a shape. A jubilee bracelet is a construction method. A sunburst dial is a finishing technique. None of these belongs to anybody, they are all decades out of any patent, and a watch wearing them is not pretending to be anything. It is a watch with a fluted bezel.

A watch wearing somebody else's name on the dial is a different object entirely. It is illegal to sell, it has no resale value, and in our experience it is built to a standard that matches the deception rather than the price. If you are unsure what you are being offered, our guide on how to spot a fake watch goes through what to check.

The short version. Buying the look is legitimate and always has been. Buying the name is not. Everything in this article is the first thing, and nothing we sell carries a name that is not its own.

Frequently Asked Questions

What actually makes a cheap watch look expensive?

Six details do most of the work: a fluted bezel, a five-link jubilee-style bracelet, a sunburst dial, a blue dial, an integrated bracelet, and two-tone steel and gold. All six are shapes and finishes rather than materials, which is why they transfer honestly to an affordable watch. A watch with two or three of them will read as far more costly than its price, and none of it involves pretending to be another brand.

Is a fluted bezel on a 70 euro watch the same as on a luxury watch?

The shape is the same and it is machined properly. What differs is everything behind it: the crystal, the movement and the finishing tolerances. A fluted bezel started life in the 1920s as a way to screw a case down watertight with a tool, so it is a manufacturing pattern, not a trademark. Buying a watch that has one is not buying an imitation of anything.

Is it wrong to buy a watch that looks like an expensive one?

No, and the distinction matters. A design cue like a fluted bezel or a jubilee bracelet is decades out of patent and belongs to nobody, so a watch using one is simply a watch with that design. A watch carrying another company's name on the dial is a counterfeit: illegal to sell, worthless to resell, and usually built to match the deception rather than the price. Buy the look, never the name.

Does a blue dial cost more than a black one?

Not at our prices. Blue was historically harder to produce consistently, which is where its more-expensive reputation comes from, but that stopped being true some time ago. We have seven blue-dialled watches from 5.800 to 8.600 Lek and they cost the same as their black-dialled equivalents. If you prefer blue, there is no premium to pay for it.

Will the gold-tone wear off a two-tone watch?

Gold-tone at this price is plating over steel, and plating does wear eventually, but slowly and mostly at the clasp and bracelet edges where it rubs. Keep it away from perfume, cleaning products and sea water and it holds up for years. If you want gold colour that will never wear, that is solid gold and it is a different category of purchase entirely.
